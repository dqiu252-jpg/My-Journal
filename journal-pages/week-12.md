## Project Statement
**Trapped by Data**

I check my phone dozens of times each day. Many of these interactions happen unconsciously: a quick glance during a pause in conversation, a habitual reach while waiting, or a momentary response to boredom. This project transforms those seemingly insignificant actions into a physical data visualisation that makes an invisible behaviour visible.

The installation consists of two boards, each centred around a mobile phone. The first board is built from my own self-collected data gathered over a week. Every time I unconsciously checked my phone, I recorded the interaction by adding a coloured thread to the structure. The second board is created collectively by visitors during the exhibition. Participants are invited to reflect on the last time they checked their phone and contribute their own thread to the growing network. Together, these boards form a comparison between personal and collective behavioural data.

The work explores a future in which everyday actions are continuously recorded, measured, and transformed into data. Digital technologies increasingly monitor behaviour that often occurs without conscious awareness. In this scenario, even small gestures become data points that can be analysed, categorised, and interpreted. Rather than focusing on large datasets, this project examines one ordinary habit and asks what can be revealed when it is made visible.

The visualisation engages critically with ideas of data humanism by treating data as personal, subjective, and embodied. Inspired by projects such as Dear Data, it rejects the assumption that data must be numerical, objective, or screen-based. Instead, thread, accumulation, and physical labour become methods of recording experience. As the structure grows denser, the phone gradually becomes obscured by the data it generates, creating a visual metaphor for the relationship between technology, behaviour, and surveillance.

By inviting audiences to contribute their own data, the work shifts from observation to participation. It asks viewers to recognise their own habits within the installation and reflect on how small, unconscious actions accumulate into larger systems of personal data that increasingly shape contemporary life.
