## Journal review

**Challenges Faced During the Project**

1. Defining a Clear Behaviour to Track
One of the biggest challenges was deciding what data to collect. My initial focus on attention and distraction was too broad, making it difficult to create a consistent recording method. After feedback, I refined the project to focus specifically on unconscious phone-checking behaviour, which gave the project a clearer direction.

2. Translating Data into a Physical Form
Another challenge was finding an effective way to represent behavioural data physically. Early experiments used a variety of materials, colours, and objects, but this made the visual language confusing. Through prototyping, I learned that simplifying the material system helped make the data easier to understand while still maintaining its complexity.

3. Balancing Personal Data and Audience Engagement
As the project developed, I realised that the work was heavily focused on my own behaviour, which limited audience engagement. Feedback from classmates encouraged me to think about how visitors could participate in the data collection process. Developing a second, audience-generated board became a solution that expanded the project from a personal reflection into a collective exploration of phone habits.

4. Designing a Sustainable Data Collection Process
Recording phone interactions consistently over time proved difficult. I needed a method that was quick enough not to interrupt the behaviour itself while still capturing meaningful information such as emotional triggers and motivations. This challenge led me to experiment with different collection methods before deciding on a simplified system.

5. Resolving the Final Installation Format
Throughout the project, I explored several forms including the hanging data string, the string-board system, and the phone-trapping concept. Deciding which format best communicated the project's message while remaining practical for exhibition required ongoing testing and reflection.

### Key Decisions Made During the Project

**1. Focusing on Unconscious Phone-Checking Behaviour**
A significant decision was narrowing the project from the broad topic of attention and distraction to specifically tracking unconscious phone-checking behaviour. This created a more focused dataset and allowed me to investigate a habit that is both personal and relatable to others.

**2. Choosing Physical Data Visualisation Over Digital Visualisation**
Rather than creating a screen-based visualisation, I decided to represent the data through physical materials such as thread, pins, and boards. This decision was influenced by my interest in data humanism and physicalisation, allowing the data to become tangible and spatial rather than remaining abstract.

**3. Developing the Phone-Trapping Installation**
After exploring several prototype ideas, I decided to pursue the concept where the phone gradually becomes obscured by the data it generates. This created a stronger visual metaphor than the original hanging data string and better communicated themes of accumulation, dependence, and digital habits.

**4. Simplifying the Visual Encoding System**
Early prototypes used too many materials and variables, which made the work difficult to read. I decided to simplify the system by primarily using coloured thread and density to communicate information, making the visual language clearer and more accessible to viewers.

**5. Introducing Audience Participation**
One of the most important recent decisions was expanding the project from a personal dataset to a comparison between personal and collective behaviour. By introducing a second board where visitors can contribute their own phone-checking data during the exhibition, the project becomes more interactive and encourages audiences to reflect on their own habits alongside mine.

### Key Discoveries

**1. Accumulation Creates Meaningful Patterns**
Through prototyping, I discovered that the accumulation of thread over time was more powerful than individual data points. As the density increased, patterns of behaviour became visible, allowing viewers to understand periods of frequent phone checking without needing numerical data.

**2. Physical Data Feels More Personal Than Digital Data**
By translating behavioural data into thread, pins, and physical structures, I realised that material-based visualisation creates a stronger emotional connection than a screen-based chart. The physical growth of the work made the habit feel more tangible and real.

**3. Data Collection Is Part of the Design Process**
While experimenting with different recording methods, I discovered that the way data is collected directly influences the final outcome. This led me to treat data collection as an important design decision rather than simply a preparation stage before visualisation.

### Significant Feedback

**1. The Project Needed a More Specific Focus**
Early feedback highlighted that my original focus on "attention and distraction" was too broad. This encouraged me to narrow the project to unconscious phone-checking behaviour, which provided a clearer research question and more consistent data collection process.

**2. The Phone-Trapping Concept Was Stronger Than the Original String Idea**
Feedback from peers suggested that the idea of the phone becoming trapped by the data it generates created a more powerful visual metaphor than the hanging data string. This became a major turning point in the project's development.

**3. Audience Participation Could Strengthen the Work**
During critique sessions, several people suggested that the audience should be involved in the data collection process. This feedback inspired the idea of a second board where visitors contribute their own phone-checking data, transforming the project from a personal reflection into a collective exploration of digital habits.


## Practice Consularions

Practice round:

What is the project?

My project investigates unconscious phone-checking behaviour and explores how everyday digital habits can be translated into a physical form. I am interested in moments when I check my phone without a clear purpose, such as out of boredom, habit, or distraction. Rather than representing this behaviour through charts or screens, I use thread and physical materials to visualise the accumulation of these interactions over time.

What tools and techniques did you use?

The project uses self-collected behavioural data. I experimented with different collection methods including notebook logging, tally systems, and a data ring approach before settling on a simplified recording system. The final visualisation uses thread, pins, and a physical board. Each phone interaction is translated into a thread connection, turning behavioural data into a growing material structure.

How did the project develop?

Initially, I focused broadly on attention and distraction, but feedback helped me narrow the project to unconscious phone-checking behaviour. My first idea was a hanging data string that represented phone interactions through beads and spacing. Through prototyping and feedback, I developed the idea of the phone becoming trapped by the data it generates. This created a stronger visual metaphor and became the foundation of the project.

How does it relate to ideas about data representation?

The project is influenced by data humanism and the Dear Data project. Rather than treating data as objective numbers, it focuses on personal experiences, imperfections, and subjective observations. The work also considers how behavioural data is constantly collected in digital systems and asks viewers to reflect on their own relationship with personal data and technology.

What challenges did you encounter?

One challenge was defining a specific behaviour to track. Another was translating data into a physical form that remained readable without becoming overly complicated. I also had to simplify my material system and refine how emotional states and behaviours were represented through colour and structure.

What did you learn?

I learned that data collection is a design process in itself. The method used to collect information influences what becomes visible in the final work. I also learned that physical materials can communicate behavioural patterns differently from digital visualisations, creating a more personal and reflective experience.

What is the intended impact of the final work?

The final installation aims to make invisible digital habits visible. By comparing my own data with audience-generated data during the exhibition, the work encourages viewers to reflect on how often they interact with their phones unconsciously. I want the audience to recognise that these small actions accumulate over time and become part of a larger pattern of behaviour.
