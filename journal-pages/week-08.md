## Critical Design Propositions
Agin I had Critieques onlie with Yuhao. ### Critical Design Propositions

After discussing my project with a student from another group, I received feedback on the string-board prototype. The most interesting aspect they identified was the ability of the board to display multiple days of data simultaneously, making it easier to compare patterns and identify changes in behaviour over time. They felt this approach had greater potential than the hanging data string because it could visualise relationships between emotions, habits, and phone interactions rather than simply showing accumulation.

The least developed aspect of my project was the way emotional data was represented. While I had focused on recording phone interactions, the emotional and behavioural information behind those interactions was not yet clearly communicated. My partner suggested introducing additional visual variables such as shape, colour, and scale. For example, different shapes could represent reasons for checking the phone, while colour could represent emotional states such as distraction, attention, relaxation, or stress. The size of each shape could indicate the intensity of the feeling.

If this project were developed further, my partner suggested treating it less as a timeline and more as a behavioural map. Rather than only showing when phone interactions occur, the visualisation could reveal emotional patterns and relationships between different states throughout the day. This would create a richer representation of attention and distraction while remaining connected to my original focus on phone-checking behaviour.

This feedback encouraged me to think beyond simple data recording and consider how multiple layers of information could be encoded within the same visual system. Moving forward, I plan to experiment with combining thread, colour, shape, and spatial arrangement to create a more detailed and meaningful representation of behavioural data.

## Project Summary & Future planning
After receiving feedback on my progress report, I plan to further refine the project by narrowing the focus of the behaviour I am tracking and developing a clearer system for collecting and translating the data into physical form. Instead of broadly focusing on “attention and distraction,” I will specifically investigate moments where I instinctively reach for my phone without a strong reason or intention. I am interested in these small, unconscious gestures because they happen repeatedly throughout the day and often reflect emotional states such as boredom, avoidance, anxiety, or habit.

The next stage of the project will involve a period of self-documentation over several days. Each time I unlock or reach for my phone impulsively, I will quickly record a small amount of information, such as the approximate time, location, emotional state, or trigger. I want the recording process to remain lightweight and immediate so that it feels observational rather than analytical. I also want to preserve inconsistencies and forgotten moments within the dataset, as these gaps reflect the imperfect nature of human attention.

Alongside data collection, I will begin experimenting with materials and forms for the physical visualisation. I am currently interested in using thread, knots, beads, paper fragments, or other small found materials to create a growing suspended structure or “data string.” Each interaction with the phone will produce a physical addition to the work. I plan to test different systems of encoding, such as using colour to represent emotional states, spacing to represent time gaps, or density to show periods of repetitive behaviour. Rather than creating a fixed chart or diagram, I want the structure to feel accumulative and slightly ambiguous, allowing patterns to emerge gradually through repetition.

I also want to experiment with scale and installation. The work could exist as a hanging vertical piece, a layered arrangement across a surface, or a collection of connected strands. I am interested in how the physical growth of the structure over time might communicate accumulation more effectively than digital visualisation. As the project develops, I will document each stage through sketches, process photos, and reflections in my Making Journal.

Conceptually, I plan to deepen my engagement with ideas from data feminism and Māori data sovereignty. I want to think more critically about how personal behavioural data is interpreted, who benefits from its collection, and how systems of tracking reduce emotional or subjective experiences into simplified categories. This project is not intended to provide objective conclusions about phone usage, but rather to provoke reflection on how invisible habits become measurable and meaningful through systems of data collection.

Over the next few weeks, I will continue refining both the material system and the conceptual framing through iterative experimentation, allowing the final form to develop gradually alongside the collected data.

## Project Development & Skill Building 
### Data Collection Skill Development

One of the key challenges in this project was deciding how to collect behavioural and emotional data in a way that felt immediate, personal, and sustainable. Reflecting on my earlier Data Portrait project, I realised that the most valuable part of that exercise was not the final visualisation, but the process of observing and recording everyday experiences by hand. The act of collecting data made me more aware of behaviours that would normally go unnoticed. I wanted to bring this approach into my current project.

Before deciding on a final method, I explored several possible data collection systems.

**Option 1: Notebook Logging**

Each time I checked my phone, I would write down the time, emotional state, and reason for checking it. While this method could capture detailed information, I found that it was too slow and interrupted the behaviour I was trying to observe.

**Option 2: Tally Counter System**

I considered carrying a small card and making a mark every time I checked my phone. This method was quick and simple, but it only recorded frequency and failed to capture emotional or contextual information.

**Option 3: Data Ring Method From Week1's Data Portrait**

Inspired by my previous Data Portrait project, I experimented with using a ring-shaped collection system. Each phone interaction would be represented by a coloured bead or marker added to a circular structure. Different colours could represent emotional states, while the position of the bead around the ring could represent time. This method allowed me to record data quickly while also creating a physical trace of the behaviour itself.

After testing these approaches, I chose the Data Ring method because it combines data collection and physical visualisation within the same process. Rather than recording information separately and visualising it later, the act of collecting data directly contributes to the final artefact. This approach aligns with ideas of data humanism and physicalisation by making data collection visible, tactile, and personal. It also creates a stronger connection between behaviour and representation, allowing patterns to emerge through accumulation rather than numerical analysis.

Through this process, I developed skills in designing data collection systems, translating subjective experiences into visual variables, and critically evaluating how different methods influence the type of data that is produced. The exploration reinforced the importance of considering data collection as a design process rather than simply a preparatory stage before visualisation.
